How to use this coursebook

This coursebook supports the HR English course for HR professionals. The five modules follow the working life of one international employee, Daniel, from his job interview to his exit interview. Each module has a short text on the topic with key words, a reading with dialogues and language points, topic cards, useful phrases and expressions, common errors for Finnish speakers, a learning journal task and a quiz. The Vocabulary tab covers the main areas of HR work in English and Finnish.

Study each module before the class it belongs to: Module 1 before class 2, Module 2 before class 3, and so on. Write your learning journal entry after the class. The Accommodation tab explains how to adjust your English to your listener. Read it before Module 1; it supports all modules. The Final presentation tab explains the task for the last class.

  1. Read the short text. Study the key words and phrases.
  2. Read the dialogues. Study the numbered language points.
  3. Read the topic cards. Say the useful phrases aloud.
  4. Check the common errors.
  5. Add a short entry to your learning journal.
  6. Do the module quiz on the Quiz tab.

When you have finished all modules, do the "All modules" quiz.

Modules

Communication skills

Communication accommodation

How do we adjust our English to the person we are talking to, and how much is too much? This section explains communication accommodation and shows how to use it in HR conversations. It supports all five modules.

Text: Adjusting to your listener

When we talk, we adjust how we speak to the person in front of us. We speak more slowly to a learner, choose simpler words for a newcomer, or start using a colleague's expressions. The social psychologist Howard Giles and his colleagues called this communication accommodation1. Their Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT) has been developed since the 1970s.

The theory describes three main strategies. In convergence2, speakers adjust towards each other, for example in speed, vocabulary or formality. Convergence usually signals that we want to be understood and to get closer. In divergence3, a speaker stresses the difference, for example with more jargon or a stronger accent, often to show a separate identity. In maintenance4, a speaker simply continues in their own style.

Accommodation can also go wrong. Over-accommodation5 means adjusting too much: speaking very slowly, loudly or simply to someone who does not need it. It can sound patronising6. Under-accommodation7 means not adjusting enough: fast speech, idioms and local references that the listener cannot follow.

For HR professionals, accommodation is a practical skill. Many conversations at work happen in English as a lingua franca8: both speakers use English as a second or third language. What matters most is not native-like English but mutual intelligibility9: clear speech, checking understanding and adjusting to the listener. Native speakers are not automatically easy to understand. Fast speech and idioms can make them hard to follow, so they need to accommodate too.

Key words and phrases
  1. communication accommodation: adjusting how you speak to your listener
  2. convergence: adjusting towards the other speaker
  3. divergence: stressing the difference from the other speaker
  4. maintenance: keeping your own style without adjusting
  5. over-accommodation: adjusting too much; it can sound patronising
  6. patronising: treating someone as less able than they are (AmE: patronizing)
  7. under-accommodation: not adjusting enough for the listener
  8. English as a lingua franca: English used between people who have different first languages
  9. mutual intelligibility: both speakers can understand each other
Dialogues: Three ways to explain change negotiations

Laura, the HR manager, explains the upcoming change negotiations to Daniel. Read three versions.

Version A

Laura: Right, so heads-up: we're kicking off the YT process next week, well, muutosneuvottelut now, and the TES stuff means we'll have to touch base with the luottamusmies before we circle back to the teams. Ballpark, six weeks. Makes sense?

Daniel: Er… yes.

Version B

Laura: Daniel. We. Have. Change. Negotiations. Do you know what negotiations are? It is when people talk. About changes. OK?

Daniel: Yes, I know what negotiations are.

Version C

Laura: Daniel, I'd like to tell you about something important. Next week, we're starting change negotiations. In Finnish, they're called1 muutosneuvottelut. It's a process that the law requires2 before an organisation reduces staff or makes big changes.

Daniel: Does that mean people will lose their jobs?

Laura: That's a fair question.3 It's possible, but nothing has been decided. The negotiations will take about six weeks. During that time, we'll talk to the shop steward, that is, the union's representative here4, and then to the teams. Please stop me if anything is unclear.5

Daniel: I think it's clear. But what does it mean for my team?

Laura: Let me put it another way.6 Your team isn't the focus of these negotiations, but everyone will get the same information at the same time. Just to check7: what will you tell your team if they ask you?8

Daniel: That nothing is decided yet, and that we'll get information in the next six weeks.

Laura: Exactly.

Version A under-accommodates: fast speech, idioms (heads-up, touch base, circle back, ballpark) and Finnish abbreviations. Daniel says "yes", but he has probably not understood. Version B over-accommodates: it is slow, broken and patronising. Daniel is a professional who understands English well. Version C converges: clear structure, Finnish terms explained, no idioms, and a real check of understanding.

Language points
  1. In Finnish, they're called: introduces a Finnish term and connects it to English
  2. It's a process that the law requires: explains the term in plain words
  3. That's a fair question.: acknowledges a worry before answering
  4. that is, the union's representative here: explains a term without stopping the sentence
  5. Please stop me if anything is unclear.: invites the listener to ask
  6. Let me put it another way.: reformulates when the first explanation may not have worked
  7. Just to check: introduces a check of understanding
  8. what will you tell your team if they ask you?: checks understanding with an open question, not "Do you understand?"
Accommodation in practice

Adjust to your listener, not to a rule. Watch the reactions, and adjust again.

Instead of…Try…
fast speech without pausesa slightly slower pace and a pause between ideas
long sentences with several ideasone idea per sentence
idioms: touch base, ballpark, circle backplain words: contact, about, return to
abbreviations: TES, YT, TTHthe full English term, plus the Finnish name once
"Do you understand?"an open check: "What will you do first?"
numbers and dates only in speechnumbers and dates also in writing
very slow, loud or simplified speechnormal speech, clearly structured
Useful phrases: when you speak
  • Let me put it another way.
  • In other words, …
  • What I mean is…
  • Sorry, I'm speaking too fast.
  • Please stop me if anything is unclear.
  • In Finnish, this is called… In English, we say…
  • I'll send you a short summary in writing.
Useful phrases: when you listen
  • Sorry, could you say that again?
  • Could you speak a bit more slowly, please?
  • Sorry, what does "ballpark" mean?
  • Just to check: do you mean…?
  • Could you write that down for me?
In short: Converge towards your listener. Avoid idioms and abbreviations, explain Finnish terms and check understanding with open questions.

Final presentation

The task

Give a short talk (4–5 minutes) for new international employees at your organisation. Choose one topic that new employees need to understand, for example:

  • annual leave and how it builds up
  • occupational health care
  • sick leave: what to do when you are ill
  • the development discussion
  • the collective agreement
  • feedback and communication culture in Finnish workplaces
  • how your organisation and HR team are organised

After your talk, answer one or two questions from the audience.

Structure

  1. Opening: welcome the audience and say what you will cover.
  2. Main part: two or three main points. Use the four steps: rule, reason, what to do, where to find more.
  3. Summary: repeat the key message in one or two sentences.
  4. Questions: invite questions and check understanding.
Useful phrases
  • Welcome, everyone. Today I'd like to explain how annual leave works in Finland.
  • I'll cover three things: …
  • First, … / Let's move on to … / Finally, …
  • The main difference from many other countries is that…
  • In practice, this means that…
  • So, to sum up, …
  • Do you have any questions? / Does that make sense?
  • That's a good question. I'm not sure, but I'll find out and let you know.

Checklist

  • My talk is 4–5 minutes long.
  • I use plain English and explain every Finnish term.
  • I use signposts to show the structure.
  • I have checked the facts in our own policies or official sources.
  • I have practised the talk aloud at least once.
Use Modules 2 and 3 to prepare. Add a short learning journal entry after the talk: what went well, and what you would change.
